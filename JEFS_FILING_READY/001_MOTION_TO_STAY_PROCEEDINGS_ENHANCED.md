**2. Void Ab Initio Custody Order — Lack of Statutory and Procedural Foundation**

*Reference: Dropbox/CASE_EVIDENCE/SECOND_MOTION_SHOW_CAUSE.pdf*

The June 24, 2025 custody order is **void ab initio** (void from inception) for the following independent grounds:

- **No parenting plan**: HRS §571-46(b) mandates a detailed parenting plan specifying custody schedule, visitation, transportation, holiday allocation, and decision-making authority. The June 24 order **contains zero specificity** on any of these points. A custody order without a parenting plan is **per se defective and void.**

- **No psychological evaluation**: HRS §571-46(b) explicitly requires that courts "shall consider...the results of any court-ordered evaluation of any party." **Zero psychological evaluations** were ordered, sought, or completed. The custody order thus **fails mandatory statutory compliance.**

- **No best-interest analysis**: HRS §571-46(b) requires consideration of 16 specific statutory factors (parental relationship, stability, abuse history, children's preference, substance abuse, mental health, availability, etc.). The June 24 order **contains zero analysis** of any statutory factor. The order is **facially deficient as a matter of law.**

**Legal consequence:**
- *In re Doe*, 100 Haw. 335 (1997): Family court orders failing to comply with HRS §571-46(b) are reversible on appeal as matter of law.
- A void order cannot support appellate affirmance; it must be vacated.
- Staying proceedings is appropriate when the underlying order is void.

### III. LEGAL STANDARD FOR STAY PENDING APPEAL

Under HFCR 62(a), HFCR 73, and Hawaii appellate jurisprudence, a stay pending appeal may be granted where:

1. **Substantial legal questions exist** (movant has raised non-frivolous issues likely to result in reversal);
2. **Irreparable harm will result absent stay** (loss of non-compensable interests);
3. **Balance of hardships favors granting stay** (public interest and equity considerations);
4. **Likelihood of success on appeal** (likelihood that movant will prevail on the merits).

*See* **RCUH v. PJDC**, 91 Haw. 335 (App. 2000); **Amical v. Amical**, 123 Haw. 410 (2010).

**All four factors are satisfied in the present case with exceptional strength.**

### V. IRREPARABLE HARM — DETAILED ANALYSIS

**Irreparability requires injury that cannot be fully cured by money damages or post-appellate relief.**

**1. Parent-Child Relationship (Non-fungible interest)**

The psychological, emotional, and relational bond between parent and child is **unique and irreplaceable.** Daily separation during the pendency of appeal causes:
- **Developmental harm** to minor child (loss of paternal presence during formative years)
- **Psychological injury** to Plaintiff (severance of fundamental relational interest)
- **Temporal loss** (time cannot be recovered or compensated)

*Santosky v. Kramer*, 455 U.S. 745 (1982): "[T]he parental interest in retaining custody of children is sufficiently protected by the heightened standard of proof [because] the parent-child relationship is an interest of the highest order, and therefore may not be disregarded in the absence of unusually important countervailing interests."

**Corollary principle**: If the parent-child relationship is "interest of the highest order" at the deprivation stage, it is equally "interest of the highest order" at the appellate stage. Continued deprivation pending appeal causes irreparable harm to this fundamental interest.

**2. Physical Safety of Minor Child**

*Federal Exhibit 1FDV-23-0001009_EX-AUDIO-001* documents Defendant's admission of untreated bone fracture. Pediatric fractures left untreated create risk of:
- **Compartment syndrome** (vascular compromise; potential loss of limb)
- **Nerve damage** (permanent sensory/motor deficit)
- **Growth disturbance** (long-bone fractures in children affect growth plates)
- **Chronic pain** (malunion; functional disability)

Each day of continued custody with Defendant increases risk of **recurrent medical neglect** and physical injury.

**HRS §587A-4(a)**: Medical neglect is defined as failure to provide necessary medical treatment. Plaintiff seeks to protect child by obtaining custody; Plaintiff's deprivation of custody directly enables ongoing medical neglect.

**Irreparability**: Child's physical integrity and long-term health cannot be restored after injury. Plaintiff is the protective party; Plaintiff's deprivation of custody is directly causative of irreparable harm to child.

**3. Loss of Appellate Remedy if Decree Becomes Final**

Hawaii Family Court Rules provide **30-day period** for entry of final order. If Decree becomes final without stay pending appeal:
- Appellate Court's flexibility in granting relief diminishes
- Parties become locked into custody arrangements with reduced availability of modification
- Equitable remedies become more difficult to grant
- Status quo becomes entrenched

**Corollary**: Staying proceedings preserves the full spectrum of appellate remedies and prevents dilution of relief through passage of time and entrenchment of unlawful status quo.
